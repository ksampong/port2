Background: This project is the result of multiple interests I've had over time, including low-level computing and processor design. It ties back to my fascination with how computer architecture operates at the most fundamental level. The motivation to pursue this came from realizing that I lacked a deep technical understanding of many of my interests in electronics and programming. I wanted to bridge that gap with hands-on experience and decided to work on projects that would challenge me.

Motivation: About a year ago, I felt the need to build technical skills that could match my wide-ranging interests. I realized that while I had a lot of theoretical knowledge, I wasn’t fully equipped to bring my ideas to life. To remedy this, I started taking on projects and exploring textbooks in areas where I felt weak. The first project I took up was inspired by Ben Eater's YouTube series on building a 6502-based computer. However, after gathering some parts and getting started, I quickly realized my abilities were not where I wanted them to be, and the project stalled.

Journey: After the initial setback, I decided to pivot my focus to emulating the 6502 processor. I started by writing the CPU and memory behavior, initially copying instruction set behavior from 6502.org. While doing so, I didn’t fully understand the underlying principles, but I gained a better grasp as I continued. Over time, I worked on implementing all the addressing modes and the fetch-execute cycle, which really helped me understand how these low-level systems operate.

After making significant progress with the emulator, I managed to test a handful of instructions and eventually completed the entire instruction set. This journey has taught me not only how to emulate hardware but also how to structure a project effectively and persist through challenges. While I’m now able to emulate the 6502 processor's behavior with reasonable accuracy, my next steps involve unit testing to ensure everything works as expected and possibly developing tools like an instruction visualizer or a compiler for the emulator.

Technical Breakdown:

  • CPU Core: The CPU core in the emulator models the 6502's registers (A, X, Y, and status flags), and its cycles are based on the fetch-decode-execute cycle. The execution logic handles each instruction's opcode and manages the status flags for carry, zero, negative, and overflow.
  • Memory System: I’ve emulated the 6502’s memory model, including the zero-page (addresses 0-255), stack, and the full 64KB address space. Memory management is key to the functioning of the processor, as many instructions rely on specific memory regions.
  • Instruction Execution: I’ve implemented the full 6502 instruction set, including arithmetic, logic, comparison, and control flow operations. Each instruction updates the processor’s flags appropriately, based on the operation’s result.
  • Addressing Modes: All of the 6502’s addressing modes have been implemented, including direct, indirect, indexed, and relative addressing. This gives the emulator flexibility in handling different types of operands.
  • Emulation Cycle: The emulator follows a fetch-decode-execute cycle to simulate the actual process of instruction execution. Each instruction is fetched from memory, decoded, and executed in sequence, mimicking the behavior of the original 6502 processor.
  • Testing: I’ve written test cases to verify the correct execution of several 6502 instructions. Currently, the testing is still in progress, with a focus on ensuring accuracy in how flags are set and how each instruction behaves.
  • Future Work: Moving forward, I plan to implement cycle-accurate emulation, which will allow for a more detailed representation of the 6502’s timing. I also plan to develop debugging tools, such as a visualizer for instructions, and explore the possibility of creating a simple compiler to work with the emulator.

Background: This project was part of my digital design coursework, where I designed and implemented a fully functional 4-bit Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU) using VHDL and deployed it on an FPGA (Nexys 3). The hierarchical, modular approach ensured that each component was reusable and well-structured.

Motivation: While working through my digital design course, I saw this project as the perfect opportunity to reinforce and expand my hardware design skills. Rather than just completing it as an assignment, I took the time to refine my approach, ensuring I deeply understood the design principles involved. It allowed me to bridge theory with hands-on implementation, gaining experience in modular VHDL coding, FPGA synthesis, and debugging complex digital systems.

Journey: The design process started with breaking the ALU into smaller, manageable components:

  • Half Adder & Full Adder: The core arithmetic units, responsible for basic addition operations.
  • 1-Bit Logic Unit: Implemented bitwise operations (AND, OR, XOR, NOT).
  • 1-Bit Arithmetic Unit: Combined adders and multiplexers to enable addition, subtraction, and direct pass-through operations.
  • 4-Bit ALU Composition: Integrated the smaller units into a full 4-bit ALU, with multiplexers selecting between arithmetic and logic operations.
  • Seven-Segment Display Output: Designed a decoder to convert the 4-bit binary result into human-readable digits for FPGA output.

Once the core ALU was functional, I synthesized it and tested it on the Nexys 3 FPGA, verifying correct operation in real-time. Debugging timing issues and ensuring proper carry propagation in arithmetic operations proved to be valuable learning experiences.

Key Takeaways:

  • Developed modular and reusable VHDL components, reinforcing hierarchical digital design principles.
  • Gained hands-on experience with FPGA synthesis, debugging, and real-time hardware testing.
  • Strengthened my understanding of control logic and combinational vs. sequential circuit behavior.

Future Work: I plan to extend this project by adding multiplication and division functionality and exploring optimizations like carry-lookahead adders to improve performance. Another long-term goal is integrating this ALU into a simple FPGA-based CPU.

Like many inspired by science fiction, I've always had a deep interest in Artificial Intelligence (AI). Throughout high school, I made several attempts to understand the field, with notable influences including 3Blue1Brown’s video series on machine learning and various popular science explainers. As I learned more, the landscape became clearer, and I was able to define a path for myself, as well as identify the destination I aimed to reach: understanding machine learning as a tool for problem-solving and analyzing complex systems.

First Attempt: Building a Neural Network from Scratch

My first practical attempt was to build a neural network from scratch—nothing fancy, just a proof of concept. My plan was simple: code a neuron, then a layer, and eventually a small network, based on what I had learned from YouTube. While creating the neuron and layer objects was straightforward, I quickly realized I didn’t know how to properly use them. Tasks like connecting neurons, passing signals from layer to layer, and adding cost functions proved more challenging than I had anticipated.

Fortunately, a friend introduced me to Kaggle, which turned out to be a game-changer.

Kaggle: The Turning Point

Kaggle provided a clear roadmap through its Kaggle Learn courses. I started with the introductory course on machine learning, where I was introduced to the pandas and scikit-learn libraries, and gained an understanding of data preparation. It was here that I realized machine learning was as much about analytics and statistics as it was about programming. I continued with the intermediate course, and while I found machine learning to be somewhat unintuitive, I became increasingly determined to master it because of its immense value. I also completed the pandas course to improve my data handling skills. Feeling ready for more, I moved on to deep learning, where I was introduced to TensorFlow and Keras. Finally, I was able to achieve my initial goal of building a functional neural network. I also explored computer vision through Kaggle's courses to further broaden my skills.

Internship Experience: Applying What I Learned

After my Kaggle courses, I wanted to apply what I had learned practically, so I began searching for internships. I landed a role with Stanbic (Standard) Bank Ghana in their software engineering department, where I was fortunate to work on an exploratory research project involving natural language processing (NLP). This experience deepened my understanding of machine learning and NLP in real-world applications, giving me a clearer vision of how to approach similar challenges in the future.

Books

1. "Introduction to Classical and Quantum Computing" by Thomas Wong

Motivated by my growing interest in quantum computing, I began reading *Introduction to Classical and Quantum Computing* by Thomas Wong to build a solid foundation in both classical and quantum computing. My primary goal is to gain an intuitive understanding of quantum gates and algorithms. I have found the explanations in this book to be clear, and it’s been a valuable resource for understanding the underlying principles of quantum mechanics as they apply to computing.

I am currently on Chapter 4, titled *Multiple Quantum Bits*, with a focus on Section 4.4.3, which explores the Toffoli gate. This section deepens my understanding of how quantum operations can be extended to multiple qubits, providing insights into quantum control and entanglement. The chapter builds on the foundation laid in earlier chapters, which introduced classical computation, quantum gates, and quantum algorithms.

Key Chapters Covered So Far:

  • Chapter 1: Classical Computation – A review of classical computation principles, providing context for quantum computation.
  • Chapter 2: Quantum Bits – Introduction to qubits, superposition, and entanglement, which are essential for understanding quantum computation.
  • Chapter 3: Quantum Gates – A deep dive into how quantum gates manipulate qubits to implement quantum algorithms, forming the basis of quantum logic operations.
  • Chapter 4: Multiple Quantum Bits – Expands on quantum gates to multiple qubits, with Section 4.4.3 focusing on the Toffoli gate, an essential operation for quantum control.

My intention is to finish this book before moving on to *Quantum Computation and Quantum Information* by Michael A. Nielsen and Isaac L. Chuang. This second textbook offers a more advanced treatment of quantum computing concepts, and I believe it will complement the foundational knowledge I've gained from Wong’s book.
